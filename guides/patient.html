Kidney Health, Gout & Blood Pressure

A plain-language, evidence-based guide to chronic kidney disease (CKD), gout, and hypertension—conditions that often overlap and need coordinated care.

Evidence reviewed October 24, 2026
How to use this guide. This is education for patients and a quick reference for clinicians, not a diagnosis or a personal treatment plan. Medication doses, food limits, blood-pressure goals, and dialysis decisions depend on the person’s labs, other conditions, medicines, and preferences. Use the links below for the full guidance and confirm decisions with the treating clinician.
Get urgent help when needed.

Blood pressure: if a reading is over 180/120 mm Hg, wait one minute and repeat it. If it remains that high, contact a clinician immediately. Call emergency services for chest pain, shortness of breath, weakness/numbness, vision change, severe back pain, or trouble speaking.

Sudden hot, red, swollen joint: fever or feeling very ill can mean joint infection, which can look like gout and needs urgent evaluation. Do not assume a first attack is gout.

Advanced CKD: severe breathlessness, confusion, fainting, chest pain, inability to keep fluids down, or rapidly worsening swelling needs urgent medical assessment.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

CKD means kidney damage or reduced kidney function that persists for at least 3 months. It can be silent for years, so blood and urine testing are important—especially with diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, or a family history of kidney failure.

For patients: Ask for both your eGFR (a blood-test estimate of filtering) and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). One result is only a snapshot; clinicians interpret repeat results over time and consider the cause.

Stages and what they mean

These are GFR categories. CKD stage also depends on albumin in urine and other evidence of kidney damage: G1 or G2 alone does not establish CKD if there are no other signs of damage.

Albuminuria categories: A1 UACR <30 mg/g; A2 30–300 mg/g; A3 >300 mg/g. Higher albuminuria generally signals greater kidney and cardiovascular risk, even at the same eGFR.

Common causes and evaluation

Common causes

Diabetes and high blood pressure are frequent causes. Other causes include glomerular or autoimmune disease, inherited conditions such as polycystic kidney disease, repeated obstruction/infection, and medication or toxin injury.

Core monitoring

Serum creatinine/eGFR, UACR, blood pressure, medication review, and relevant blood tests (for example potassium, bicarbonate, hemoglobin, calcium, and phosphate as CKD advances). Imaging or other tests depend on the suspected cause.

Treatment by stage: protect function and treat complications

  • Across G1–G5: identify and treat the cause; manage diabetes, blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking, and cardiovascular risk; keep vaccines and preventive care current; review all prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, supplements, and herbal products.
  • When albuminuria or another indication is present: an ACE inhibitor or ARB may reduce albuminuria and protect kidneys. Potassium and creatinine need monitoring after starting or increasing the dose. Do not combine ACE inhibitors and ARBs unless a specialist has a specific reason.
  • For eligible adults: KDIGO recommends an SGLT2 inhibitor for type 2 diabetes with CKD and eGFR ≥20; for CKD with eGFR ≥20 and UACR ≥200 mg/g or heart failure; and suggests considering it for eGFR 20–45 with lower UACR. Eligibility, contraindications, sick-day/surgery instructions, and monitoring are clinician decisions.
  • G3–G5: adjust medication doses to kidney function and monitor for anemia, acidosis, potassium problems, fluid overload, and CKD-related mineral/bone disorder. Refer to nephrology for advanced disease, rapid decline, severe albuminuria, uncertain cause, resistant hypertension, or difficult complications.
  • G4–G5: discuss hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, transplant, and conservative kidney care early enough to make an informed plan. Dialysis is not started from an eGFR number alone; symptoms, complications, labs, and patient goals matter.
Clinician note: Track both GFR and albuminuria, confirm chronicity, estimate progression risk, and distinguish CKD progression from acute kidney injury. KDIGO’s SGLT2 thresholds above summarize key adult indications and should be checked against the full current guideline and individual product labeling.

Food and drink: personalize, don’t over-restrict

There is no single “renal diet.” Restrictions are based on kidney function, blood tests, urine findings, blood pressure, fluid balance, nutrition status, medications, and dialysis modality. A renal dietitian can help keep meals safe and enjoyable.

Usually useful foundations

  • Favor minimally processed foods and a heart-healthy pattern with vegetables, fruit, grains, and appropriate protein.
  • Reduce excess sodium, especially packaged meals, processed meats, salty snacks, instant noodles, and salty sauces.
  • Choose unsaturated fats more often than saturated/trans fats.

Limit only when indicated

  • Potassium: restrict specific high-potassium foods only when labs/medicines require it. Avoid potassium-based salt substitutes unless cleared.
  • Phosphorus: check labs; processed foods with ingredients containing “phos” can add highly absorbable phosphate.
  • Fluids: a fluid limit is not needed by everyone. It may be prescribed for reduced urine output, swelling, heart failure, or dialysis.
  • Protein: avoid high-protein supplements/diets unless advised. Needs differ before and during dialysis; dialysis can increase protein needs.
Food-safety note: Do not use a blanket list that bans bananas, tomatoes, beans, dairy, whole grains, or water for every person with CKD. The right limits depend on measured potassium/phosphorus, nutrition, treatment, and fluid status.

Common kidney-safe habit: ask before using NSAID pain relievers such as ibuprofen or naproxen, especially with CKD, dehydration, heart failure, or blood-pressure medicines. Never stop a prescribed kidney or blood-pressure medicine without discussing it.

Gout

Gout is inflammatory arthritis caused by monosodium urate crystals. It often begins suddenly—classically in the big toe—with intense pain, warmth, redness, and swelling. Repeated attacks can damage joints and form tophi.

For patients: A high uric acid blood result alone does not prove gout, and a normal result during a flare does not always rule it out. A clinician may sample joint fluid to look for crystals, especially when the diagnosis is uncertain.
Urgent assessment: a first hot swollen joint, fever, feeling very ill, rapidly worsening symptoms, or an immunocompromised state needs prompt medical evaluation because joint infection can resemble gout.

What can trigger or accompany gout?

Risk rises when urate stays high; the kidneys may not remove enough urate. CKD, family history, obesity, hypertension, heart disease, diuretics, alcohol, and some dietary patterns can contribute. Review possible medicine contributors with a prescriber—do not stop a diuretic or low-dose aspirin on your own.

Treatment

During a flare

Evidence-based first-line options include colchicine, an NSAID, or an oral/injected glucocorticoid. Choice depends on kidney function, stomach bleeding risk, heart failure, diabetes, drug interactions, and other factors. For CKD, KDIGO favors low-dose colchicine or oral/intra-articular glucocorticoids over NSAIDs; advanced CKD requires clinician-directed dosing.

Preventing future flares

Urate-lowering therapy (ULT) is strongly recommended for tophi, gout-related joint damage, or frequent flares (≥2/year). Allopurinol is usually preferred first line, including in CKD, but start low and titrate under clinician supervision. ACR uses a treat-to-target serum urate below 6 mg/dL; temporary flare-prevention medicine is often used when ULT starts.

  • ULT may also be considered after a first flare when CKD stage 3 or worse, kidney stones, or serum urate >9 mg/dL is present. Guidelines differ in emphasis; discuss benefits and risks.
  • Do not start, stop, or change allopurinol, febuxostat, colchicine, or steroid treatment without a prescriber. Severe rash, facial swelling, breathing trouble, or other serious reaction needs emergency care.
  • For an active flare, follow the personal flare plan early. Do not self-treat with NSAIDs if you have CKD unless your clinician specifically says they are safe for you.

Food and drink with gout

Diet changes can reduce risk and support overall health, but their average effect on urate is modest; they do not replace ULT when medication is indicated.

Limit or avoid when possible

  • Beer and spirits; alcohol can trigger flares. Ask about a safe approach, especially with frequent attacks or interacting medicines.
  • Sugar-sweetened drinks and foods with high-fructose corn syrup.
  • Frequent large portions of organ meats, red/processed meat, and some seafood (such as anchovies, sardines, shellfish).

Choose more often

  • A balanced DASH- or Mediterranean-style pattern, with vegetables, whole grains, and suitable low-fat dairy if tolerated and appropriate for CKD.
  • Gradual weight reduction if overweight; avoid crash diets and prolonged fasting.
  • Water according to thirst and the clinician’s fluid plan. CKD or heart failure may require a fluid limit.

Plant foods that contain purines (such as beans and lentils) are not automatically forbidden. The ACR notes that food-specific effects are generally small; focus on overall pattern, alcohol, fructose-sweetened drinks, weight, and taking prescribed ULT consistently.

Clinician note: serum urate target <6 mg/dL is the ACR treat-to-target goal. Start allopurinol at a low dose (lower in CKD) and titrate using serial urate measurements; assess HLA-B*58:01 testing in populations for whom ACR recommends it. Review interactions (notably azathioprine/mercaptopurine) and prophylaxis renal dosing.

High blood pressure (hypertension)

Blood pressure is written as systolic over diastolic, in mm Hg. High blood pressure often has no symptoms; diagnosis uses repeated, correctly measured readings rather than one isolated number.

For most adults receiving treatment, the general 2025 U.S. goal is below 130/80 mm Hg, individualized for pregnancy, frailty, symptoms, limited life expectancy, other conditions, and tolerability. Medication is generally recommended at average BP ≥140/90; at ≥130/80 it is recommended for selected adults with cardiovascular disease, prior stroke, diabetes, CKD, or elevated predicted cardiovascular risk, alongside lifestyle care.

How age and sex affect the numbers

  • Adults: the U.S. adult diagnostic categories above are the same for men and women. Age and sex affect risk assessment and individualized treatment decisions, but do not create separate adult cutoffs.
  • Children younger than 13: clinicians compare readings with age-, sex-, and height-specific percentiles. A pediatric clinician should interpret the table and confirm elevated readings.
  • Age 13 and older: U.S. pediatric guidance generally uses fixed cutoffs similar to adult categories; diagnosis still requires appropriate repeat measurements and clinical assessment.
  • Pregnancy and postpartum: pregnancy-related hypertension and preeclampsia need obstetric assessment and pregnancy-specific thresholds/medications; do not use this general adult table alone.
  • Older adults: no automatic “normal BP rises with age” rule applies. Goals should account for standing BP, dizziness/falls, frailty, kidney and heart conditions, and patient priorities.

Measure accurately at home

Before the reading

Use a validated upper-arm cuff of the right size. Avoid smoking, caffeine, and exercise for 30 minutes; empty your bladder; sit quietly for at least 5 minutes.

During the reading

Back supported, feet flat and uncrossed, bare arm supported at heart level, no talking. Take two readings one minute apart, at consistent times, and record both.

Bring the monitor and readings to visits. Home readings complement care; do not change medication based on one measurement unless your care team has given you a specific plan.

Treatment and daily habits

  • Heart-healthy eating (such as DASH), lower sodium, regular activity appropriate to health, healthy weight, adequate sleep, stress management, and limiting or avoiding alcohol can lower BP.
  • Common medicines include thiazide-type diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, and calcium-channel blockers. The right choice depends on age, pregnancy, kidney function, diabetes, heart disease, potassium, and other drugs.
  • With CKD and albuminuria, ACE inhibitor or ARB treatment may be kidney-protective. Clinicians monitor potassium and creatinine after changes. Potassium salt substitutes may be unsafe in CKD or with certain medicines.
  • Use prescribed medicines consistently; report side effects, dizziness, swelling, or very low readings. Do not stop treatment abruptly without medical advice.
Clinician note: The 2025 AHA/ACC adult target is <130/80 mm Hg for most adults, with individualized exceptions. KDIGO 2021 separately suggests standardized-office systolic BP <120 mm Hg for many adults with non-dialysis CKD when tolerated; that target cannot be directly applied to routine, non-standardized readings and is not a dialysis target. Clarify measurement method and patient-specific target in the care plan.
